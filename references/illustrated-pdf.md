# Illustrated PDF production

Use this for the default illustrated lecture and when updating an existing illustrated PDF.

1. Map each proposed figure to a reader question and the section where it is answered. Cover the central mechanisms, stage transitions, or empirical comparisons that benefit from a visual explanation. Choose the number of figures from the material. A teaching figure should let the reader follow an input to an output, interpret data, or explain a tradeoff. A decorative cover or a text-only flowchart is insufficient when the mechanism depends on quantities, intermediate states, or timing.
2. Use source figures when the evidence or conventions require them. Use vector circuit diagrams, plots, or schematics for technical content when feasible. Draw from verified source facts, cite the relevant passage, and label invented numbers and simplified examples. Add a caption that tells the reader what to look for. Check labels, units, arrows, encoding, and legibility at the final printed size.
3. Use a paginated layout with embedded fonts for the document language, selectable text, mathematical typesetting, consistent headings, and margins and body type suited to sustained reading. Keep captions with figures and repeat table headers as needed. Pick a PDF tool that supports the source's equations and script, such as a Unicode LaTeX engine or a browser print workflow with locally rendered math.
4. Render every final PDF page to images. Inspect the whole sequence for pagination and white space, then inspect equations, figure labels, tables, and non-Latin glyphs at readable size. Confirm text extraction and links in the exported PDF. Repair problems and rerender the affected output before delivery. Keep the editable source and the figure assets together.
5. If export fails, try another available renderer that preserves text and equations. Report any unresolved output limitation explicitly and link the editable artifact that was produced.

## Architecture figures

Use this section for architecture figures in any output format. A scientific paper in the topic area or an established notation guide is the reference for **how to draw**. The target paper, source code, configuration and released artifacts are the evidence for **what to draw**.

1. **Choose a field convention.** Inspect a representative figure or specification and record its locator. For circuits, show register or wire labels, time direction, controls, measurements and cleanup where relevant. Distinguish quantum wires from classical data or feed-forward. For models, show tensor flow, dimensions, repeated blocks, and skip or branch connections that matter. For systems, show components, interfaces, and the boundary between the implementation and its environment. Use a legend for symbols that a reader at the stated level might not know.
2. **Trace the actual implementation.** Pin the baseline by paper revision, release, commit, or artifact identity. Follow the relevant entry point, data or register declarations, and execution sequence. Record the evidence for each consequential connection, operation order, dimension, and module boundary in the figure notes. In a method with several stages, show the main intermediate values or states. Place distinct training, preprocessing, inference, verification, or submission paths in separate panels when necessary to avoid implying they execute together.
3. **Show what the evidence establishes.** When an improved public artifact extends a reproducible baseline, link each documented change to the relevant block. Treat opaque internal stages as black boxes with confirmed inputs and outputs. In the caption, identify the implementation, explain the notation, cite the relevant sources, and state the level of abstraction. Label a teaching example as a teaching example and connect its objects to the real system in the text.
4. **Check the exported figure.** Trace one input from left to right (or in the declared direction) and account for each output, including restored or cleared state when relevant. Check spatial order, labels, line endings and arrow directions against the evidence. Inspect the vector export at the final reading size for legible type, distinguishable line styles, and a caption placed with the figure. Split crowded figures into panels or use a dedicated page.
